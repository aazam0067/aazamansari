Read the investigation, correction and outcome

The problem: a working homepage hid a deeper routing issue

The customer wanted an old domain to send visitors to the corresponding pages on a new website. Redirecting the homepage alone was not enough: child pages were either failing or opening under a default hosting hostname instead of the intended custom domain. The customer asked how to achieve a server-level redirect and later provided examples showing the actual and expected destinations.

Initial diagnosis: check where the domain actually resolves

I investigated the old domain and reported that it was configured for domain-level forwarding rather than pointing to an active WordPress instance on our platform. The resolving IP addresses did not match our WP Cloud hosting setup. I explained how this particular forwarding configuration related to the reported behavior, then proposed pointing the domain to our hosting and adding it as an alternate domain on the new site.

I supplied the hosting A-record and www CNAME guidance and asked whether the customer had DNS access. When access was not immediately available, I explained that we could continue once they had it. This avoided treating a hosting-side change as sufficient before the domain setup was ready.

Follow-up diagnosis: compare a child page, not just the homepage

The customer returned with a more specific symptom: the old domain now redirected, but child pages went to the default hosting hostname. I asked for a source URL and its intended destination, and the customer supplied a service-area example. That made the remaining mismatch clear: the destination page path needed to open under the correct custom domain.

I identified that the old domain was still connected to a different account and explained why the homepage result did not establish that the child-page routing was correct. I proposed connecting the domain under the intended account and asked for confirmation before proceeding.

My action and validation

After the customer replied “Yes please,” I completed the domain connection change. I kept them informed while working, then asked them to try again and stated that the domain was connected and redirecting properly. The customer responded: “Yes its working now thank you so much!”

Skills demonstrated: Authentication troubleshooting · Escalation follow-through · Technical handoff · Customer communication

Platform fix communicated; recipient action advised; ticket closed

Read investigation and outcome

Problem and context

The partner initially reported bounced Gravity Forms notifications on 23 June, then raised an email-history visibility issue on a second website. An earlier team update described successful delivery events and a global fix for the history view. The partner briefly believed the bounces had stopped, but reported them again on 1 July. The renewed failure required separating a reporting issue from the delivery problem that was still affecting the first website.

Diagnosis and evidence

My documented involvement began on 3 July. I noted that the email was already verified in the application and checked with the team through the existing escalation. On 5 July, I followed up internally and kept the partner informed while the investigation continued. Application-level verification alone did not explain the recurring bounces.

By 9 July, the team's investigation had identified and corrected a sending-side authentication issue. In my customer update, I explained that the site's messages were now signed as the sending domain and passed DMARC. However, a second layer remained: the recipient's Mimecast security service was rejecting the messages under its impersonation/anti-spoofing policy. In this case, authentication passing did not mean the recipient's separate security policy would accept the external sending service.

My action and technical handoff

I translated the team's findings into a practical explanation of the two issues: the platform-side authentication defect had been addressed, while the remaining rejection required action from the recipient's email administrator. I explained that the website sent mail through SendGrid on the customer's behalf and that the recipient's policy was treating this as an external source using the organization's domain.

I advised the partner to have their Mimecast administrator review the identified sending source for an appropriate permitted-sender entry or impersonation-protection exception, and offered to provide the sending details needed for that review. I then moved the ticket to Waiting on Customer. This was a targeted handoff for the recipient administrator, not a claim that I had changed their security configuration.

Outcome and limits

I followed up on 12 July and marked the ticket solved. The thread documents the team-delivered authentication correction, my explanation of the remaining rejection, and the next steps provided to the partner. It does not contain confirmation that the recipient administrator applied the exception or that final delivery succeeded.
